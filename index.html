TONIGHT’S MEASUREMENT
Nothing graded C or better tonight. The screen ran over every game and no candidate cleared the bar once the matchup, the minutes and the price were all accounted for.

The grade is a function of the row, not a view about the player. It starts from how far the modelled chance sits above the 52.4% a bet needs at −110, then loses a letter for each reason to trust the number less: too few games to read him from, a disagreement with the books large enough that it has usually been our error, or a defence that has replaced most of last season’s rotation. Every deduction is named next to the row.
THE RECORD
Every pick we publish is logged with the line and price it was published at, and settled against the official NBA box score the next morning. The log is never edited after the fact — grading only adds a result to a row that already exists. Staking is flat, one unit a pick, so a good run and a lucky one cannot be made to look different.

We publish the win rate at 30 graded picks. Not before, and not later. Below that number a 3–0 start and an 0–3 start carry the same information, which is none, and showing either would be dishonest. The count and the full archive are up from pick one: see every pick in the log, or download the CSV.
THE APPROACH  ·  WHAT WE ARE BUYING
updated 23 Oct 2026

We stopped looking for mispriced lines. Across the first week every apparent edge we found turned out to be our own model’s error — a rate-times-minutes projection running about 9% high on heavy-minute players and 9% low on bench players, which is larger than any disagreement it was reporting. A seven-book consensus that has been shopped all day is not wrong by twenty points. It is not usually wrong by two.

So the question changed. The posted line sits near the median — that is what makes it a fair line. But an over is a bet on the right tail, and two players with the same fair price do not have the same tail. Scoring is bounded below at zero and unbounded above. A guard with the ball in his hands against a fast, soft defence has a different shape of night available to him than a role player with the same number next to his name.

Both curves above cross the line at exactly the same place and both clear it exactly half the time. The difference is what happens when they do. Past about 1.6 standard deviations the skewed outcome is 1.65 times as likely — the same win rate, much bigger wins. That is the whole bet, and it is why we would rather take a player’s points than his combined points-rebounds-assists: a sum of three stats averages its own variance away, which is the opposite of what we want.

So the screen looks for soft defences that have no personnel reason to improve, then for the players carrying enough of the ball to reach the tail when the game allows it. Where a defence is soft matters as much as how much: we weight each component by how reliably it repeats season to season, which turns out to mean where a team lets you shoot from persists, how well you shoot there mostly does not.

How you will know if this is nonsense.

Win rate cannot tell you. It scores a one-point miss and a blowout identically, which is exactly the distinction that matters here. So alongside the record we track the margin ratio: the average size of a win against the average size of a loss. If the thesis is real the wins run bigger and that number sits above one. If it comes back near one, the selection is finding fair coins and we will say so here rather than quietly keep going. We publish it at 25 graded picks, for the same reason we hold the win rate to 30 — before that it is noise.

PRA WATCH  ·  PLAYING TONIGHT
OUR PRA is the player’s typical points+rebounds+assists across three seasons — roughly where a book would set him, and a measure of how forecastable his minutes are, not a prediction for tonight. LOCKED means his minutes have held steady across those seasons; MOVED means they haven’t, and the PRA figure is correspondingly softer.

BOOK PRA is the combined points+rebounds+assists line the sportsbooks are quoting tonight, via The Odds API. We take the median line across books rather than the average, so one stale number can’t drag it, and we strip the vig from each book’s pair of prices before averaging them into P(OVER). The book count beside the label says how many were quoting.

VS OURS is that market line minus our figure. It is not an edge. The market price already contains tonight’s injuries, rest and matchup, none of which a three-season average knows about, so a gap is a question worth logging rather than a bet. PTS, REB and AST break the same player into his three legs. A column headed LINE is the book’s number alone; one headed LINE → PROJ also carries a Covers.com projection for that leg, which exists only for the handful of props their model chooses to flag. The heading changes rather than the two being blended, because a traded line and a model’s output are different claims.

Where no book quotes a combined line, the PRA column falls back to the three Covers projections added together and is labelled SUMMED. Book lines are never added into that sum — doing so would quietly turn a market price into a model output.
ELSEWHERE  ·  NOT OURS
Covers.com publishes its own props each night, from a writer desk and a projection model. We capture them, log every one, and grade them — because whether a free public list beats the closing line is worth knowing, and almost nobody checks.

They are not our picks and not our recommendations, and we would not bet the list. It is reference material and a benchmark to measure ourselves against. Our own card is at the top of this page.
