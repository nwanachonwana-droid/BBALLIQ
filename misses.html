Five ways we looked for an edge in totals, and didn't find one

Then a sixth test explained why the first five were the wrong question.

We started with a thesis: when the data already points toward a low-scoring game, the things nobody can see — a late scratch, bad weather, a slow whistle — tend to push the total further down rather than back toward the middle. If that's true, unders on already-low totals should be underpriced.

We built a dataset of 832 settled betting ladders across five sports and tested it five different ways.

WHAT WE TESTED RESULT
Are totals calibrated overall?Yes. No general under bias.
Do low-projected games come in under?No — slightly the opposite.
Are high totals overpriced?A point and a half gross. Gone after fees.
Does it show up for slow or defensive teams?No. Nothing in football at all.
Is the distribution's shape mispriced?The signal we found was our own bug.

That last one is worth dwelling on. The shape test produced a strong result — strong enough that we nearly believed it. It survived a date split and a significance threshold. What killed it was noticing that our settled outcomes were being computed from one set of price rungs while the distribution came from another. Two different ladders, compared against each other. Fixing that erased the finding.

A second version of the same test then produced a different strong result, on a different sport. That one was also ours: conditioning on games that landed inside the ladder without renormalising the probabilities to match. Both times, a clean-looking edge was a broken ruler.

The test that actually mattered

Having found nothing five times, we ran the model against a live slate and looked at what it could actually transact. Twenty-nine priced rungs. Every single one was rejected — not for lack of edge, but because the gap between the buy price and the sell price was wider than any edge could clear.

A model that lands two points from a fifteen-book consensus has, at best, two or three cents of theoretical edge. It cannot clear five point four. No amount of model improvement fixes that.

That also explained the phantom edges. Our historical prices were midpoints — the number halfway between the best bid and the best offer. On a thinly traded rung those two are far apart, and the midpoint is a price nobody can actually trade at. Several of the "inefficiencies" we had been chasing were just the width of a spread we'd never be able to cross.

The useful conclusion isn't that no edge exists. It's that on thin markets the question of whether you can forecast better than the crowd comes second. First you have to be able to transact at a price worth transacting at. We stopped looking for the edge and started measuring the spread.
