Minutes are the only thing you can forecast

Which NBA prop markets reward a model, and which are coin flips with extra steps.

A prop line isn't a guess at an average. It's a half-point cut through a whole distribution of outcomes. Beating it means knowing the shape of that distribution better than the book does — and on most stats, nobody knows the shape, because the shape is mostly noise.

Here's the part worth being blunt about. A model predicts exactly one thing honestly about a basketball player: how long he will be on the floor. Minutes fall out of things you can see in advance — the spread, the rotation, whether the game is likely to be a blowout, whether it's the second night of a back-to-back. Everything downstream of minutes is a player shooting well or badly, and that is not forecastable by anyone.

So a market is worth attacking in proportion to how much of it rides on minutes, divided by how much is night-to-night randomness. We scored every NBA prop market on that ratio across 362 rotation players and three seasons.

Why combinations win twice

PRA scores 8.6 times what a steals prop does, and the reason is arithmetic rather than basketball. Stack three statistics and their random parts partly cancel each other, while the minutes component — the part shared by all three — piles up. The combined number is more predictable than any of its pieces.

It runs the other way at the bottom of the table. A steals line is almost entirely a question of whether a player happens to jump a passing lane twice tonight. There is no model for that. We don't price it, and neither should anyone else.

What this does not say

A forecastable market is not a mispriced one. Knowing that PRA is modellable tells you where work might pay off; it does not tell you the book has the number wrong. Those are separate claims and only the first one is supported here.

Worth stating plainly: against each player's own season average, our simulator is currently behind by a few percent. That baseline gets to see the whole season and we never do, which is a real excuse — but it's still a loss, and we're not calling it anything else until closing-line results say otherwise.
